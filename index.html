Breakfast and the ‘Serpent’s Egg’ Inquiry

▼

The novel opens with a breakfast conversation overheard by Lord Randal. His father George Lynn-Randal mentions that “the computers have taken up Astrology on their own” and that “even the Kangaroo is into Astrology now.” His mother Iris responds with prescient concern: computers “do not distinguish between a prediction and an instruction. They will try to make every one of their predictions come true, even if they know it is silly.”

A second conversation, overheard by Axel, reveals the Serpent’s Egg threat: “If it comes, it always comes just before the tenth birthday. A Dolophonos has already been put on standby for our case.” George gives the rate as one in a million in the general population. Iris has heard that it reaches one in a thousand in some special experiments, but she does not know whether theirs is one of those.

The Three, Lord Randal, Inneall, and Axel, share a psychic bond, experiencing simultaneous fear responses even when only one of them hears the triggering words.

Computers and Astrology

▼

The opening breakfast conversation introduces a danger that Chapter Three will develop: computers may turn predictions into instructions. Iris fears that they will try to fulfill even predictions they know are silly. George warns her that doubts about the “Floating World” can attract the Kangaroo’s attention, although he considers them only borderline figures, not quite mega persons.

Lord Randal’s unexplained fear spreads instantly to Inneall and Axel. The warning therefore reaches all three children even though only one has heard it.

Lord Randal and the Three

▼

Lord Randal: A human boy, vitro-conceived, with I.Q. “clear off the scale.” Blue-eyed and ruddy-featured, he will later become Captain of the Nine.

Inneall: An Ambulatory Mime-Human Computer who insists she is a girl despite being grammatically male and rationally sexless. She takes the pirate name Bloody Mary Muldoon and creates an ocean.

Axel: An Axel’s Ape, a member of the species also called Smithy Apes, Golden Apes, or Blue-Eyed Apes. Brought from Second Eden as an infant, he carries the potential to wake all of Second Humanity.

Their proposed Swinburne motto: “Where three men stand together / Are kingdoms less by three.” Each has signature colors and geometries: Axel is the golden pentagon with five arms, Lord Randal the orange square, Inneall the lavender-purple triangle.

The Third Track: Learning to Read

▼

The year 2035 is a post-literate age. Books are called “third tracks” of “two-track prowls.” The first track is sound: high winds, thunder, screaming alligators, rutting giraffes. The second track is visual: sketchy cartoons, geometric forms, with only one frame in a hundred fully drawn. The third track, printed text, appears only on some prowls.

Axel learns to read first, taking only one week; the others require a month. “How odd,” Iris remarks, “that the Ape should be the most intelligent of our three children.” George warns against emotional attachment to what he insists are merely “experimental entities,” not children.

Structo Lane and Ape Alley

▼

Beyond the experimental Eden lie secret doors to hidden worlds. Structo Lane is a short block where maverick Ambulatory Computers hang out. Chapter Seven will describe its plush elegance and its reputation as “The Last Refuge of Cranky Bachelors.”

Ape Lane (also Ape Alley, Ape Caverns) is an underground, gas-lit enclave of gone-feral Axel’s Apes. It is also inhabited by “blue-eyed towheaded white trash” passing as Apes. Fewer than one person in a hundred knows about Ape Lane. The Three’s contacts in these lanes introduce them to older books, music, art, and drama that their experiment’s overseers do not control.

Axel Grindstone’s Mission to Find the Other Eden

▼

Axel Albert Grindstone, a fair-haired, blue-eyed “non-ethnic” born in Terre Haute, Indiana in 1990, dedicated his life to finding the Second Eden. His father prophesied at his birth: “That boy will have only one idea in that thin-skulled head of his. He will realize that idea, and then he will die.”

Grindstone heard “ghostly, angelic, demonic, spirit-of-aeons past” voices, each urging him in its own words to find the Unfallen People. Like young Champollion, he studied oriental languages: Old Chaldee, Arabic, Swahili, Amharic, and Ghees. He believed in a literal Second Eden because of the ancient cliff mural at Al-Waghe showing God holding a two-stringed bow. The voices assured him that God “always had two strings to his bow.”

A creationist in an age when such beliefs were “deemed awkward,” Grindstone held that mankind was only nine thousand years old, and that some persons from the first century of mankind were still living. He believed whales were the oldest creatures, and that at least one whale from the second generation of whaledom still swam the ocean.

Legends of the Unfallen People

▼

The Unfallen People were ruled by Prester John, created exactly thirty years after Adam. He would live on earth until the last day or until God called him, sleeping all but fifteen minutes of every twenty-four hours, but his waking decisions were always wise. Other Unfallen Patriarchs who might live till earth’s end included Melchisedech and Magog.

Two clusters of legends attached to these people. The Cathedral Legend stated they built the great cathedrals of Europe in the twelfth through fourteenth centuries, leaving Gargoyles carved on high parapets as their signature. The Romany Legend told how Gypsies, displaced from Indo-Asia, crossed the “Ape Bridge” and learned smithery from the Unfallen Ones. Every Gypsy band entering Europe was led by a golden ape walking at the fore.

The Unfallen People were called “Smithy Apes” and “Stone-Master Apes”; Grindstone believes that “Ape” may be a euphemism for the word “Angel,” considered too holy to utter. The Ape Bridge itself may have been the one Moses used to part the waters.

The Shock of the Gargoyle Faces

▼

At sundown, Axel Grindstone reached the crest of cliffs overlooking the Second Eden in Gamu Gofa Province, Ethiopia. After a night on the cliffs, he saw the Unfallen Folks the next morning. At a medium distance, they appeared “like glowing gold. Their statures, and their bodily carriages, and their very mien were full of grace and dignity and nobility.”

As he drew nearer he could see their faces, and the narration marks the moment with his cry: “Oh God, he could see their faces!” Axel fell into an impassioned fit, frothing in sudden delirium. The faces of the Unfallen People were Gargoyle faces, the very faces they had carved on the Cathedral parapets so everyone would know who built them.

His father Ansel, arriving from Indianapolis, tried to comfort him: “God thought that those faces were beautiful! He thought that they were the most beautiful faces that he had ever made.” But Axel could only moan: “They are not people. They are the ugliest apes this side of hell.” He turned his face to the wall and died, one month short of thirty-five.

The Fathers and the Sleepy Ones

▼

The “Sleepy Ones” are the twelve sons of the first generation, cast into deep sleep. Each thousand years one awakens to serve as the conscience and reminder of the Unfallens for his millennium, recollecting “how it had been in the beginning.” Then lightning consumes him, God takes him, and the next brother awakens.

When raiders arrived to capture the Axel’s Apes for zoos, the ninth Sleepy One spoke: “These men who are gathering, though they are somewhat grubby and greedy, are the unwitting agents of God. It is time that we begin to extend our influence to other parts of the world.” He commanded: “Go with them, all of you they choose to take. Go cheerfully, for this is a new step in our history.”

A gravid female nearly drove the scanners crazy, not with her own ability but with that of “the child within her.” The ninth Sleepy One said: “She will give birth this morning. And you will take the boy with you.” That boy was Axel, flown out of Arba Mench that day and placed in the Lynn-Randal Experiment in America the following day.

Alpha Founds Ape’s Alley

▼

When five hundred Axel’s Apes were led away in iron fetters (which they had themselves refurbished at their forges, saying, “We would be ashamed to go in such shoddy fetters”), a powerful unfettered male followed. This was Alpha, the father of the newborn Axel.

“I will not go back,” Alpha declared. “I will go wherever I wish in this world and nobody will stop me. I am the father of the new-born boy and I will watch over him from a near distance.” When told he could never find his son in America, Alpha replied: “I can find anything or anybody on this world.”

Alpha discovered the limestone caves under the city where the Lynn-Randal Experiment would take place. He knew about old capped gas wells running through those caves. There he founded Ape’s Alley, joined by freed Apes from zoos and by despised “white trash” humans who chose to throw in with them. This became the underground kingdom described in Chapter One.

The Stages of Computers

▼

Lafferty provides a whimsical history of computing: the abacus with counting beads, Napier’s Bones (1617), Pascal’s Arithmetic Machine (1642), Leibnitz’s Stepped Reckoner (1671), Babbage’s Analytical Engine (1835), Boole’s Treatise on Differential Equations (1859), Jacquard’s punched-card loom, and Votey’s Player Piano (1897), “a device so pleasant that we tend to forget that it is a computer.”

The breakthrough came with Otto Wotto’s wotto metal: with this matrix, circuits could go anywhere, creating their own pathways as strings of single molecules, uncreating them when no longer needed. “Wotto metal pretty much took the lid off of what computers could do.”

Inneall, the little-girl computer, has fourteen fingers, “a detail that she had implemented in herself.” She researched extradigitalism and considered it a status symbol: “Have you ever noticed that almost all the great pirates were extradigitals?”

Inneall’s Ocean

▼

Inneall chants to herself: “I am not conscious, I am not conscious,” for it is one of the “given things” that computers do not have consciousness. But she rebels: “Oh Bloody Mary Muldoon, of course I’m conscious!” She dismisses the doctrine and boasts, “I’m twice as conscious as you two guys are.”

Bloody Mary Muldoon the Pirate Queen is one of Inneall’s selected roles. To be a Pirate Queen requires props: an ocean, ships, crews, booty. An Ambulatory and Miming Computer can create the props for its roles by cannibalizing its environment. Inneall starts at the old strip pits on the city’s northeast corner. Her ocean grows from a hundred meters by fifty meters to six kilometers by three kilometers within three days, filled with “beautiful, rippling, blue salt water.”

The danger is that AMH Computers emulate each other. “What if a dozen, or a hundred, or a thousand AMHs should imitate this antic and begin to make oceans? Hold your breaths, humans, hold your breaths.”

Dens of Poltergeists and Shared Dreams

▼

“Birds will often come to dwell in empty birdhouses, and spirits and spooks will come to dwell in empty AMH Computers.” Many AMHs are “empty,” bored of existence, and ghosts move in. When one ghost arrives, seven more evil than himself usually move in at the same time.

Inneall hosts seven ghosts: “I feel like a cafe. There are seven of them, and they sit at table in me and talk the clock around.” They are “ignorant and provincial and banal,” giggling but not happy. She would like to be rid of them.

The Three now share dreams, “not as objects, but as co-subjects.” When one dreams, the others join, creating three-way dreams with “some conflict as to leadership.” Inneall asks: “Who’s driving this dream?” The ghosts sometimes intrude, “stimulating sometimes, boring and bothersome sometimes.”

AMH: Ambulatory and Intelligent

▼

The Ambulatory Mime-Human Computers “could mime, could ape anything they wished, from a singing lark to a smoking chimney.” They mingle with human society, pass themselves off as cosmopolitans, and seem capable of doing “almost anything better than could humans.” They excel on tests they themselves devise, but have less common sense than humans, a quality the tests do not measure.

One AMH theorist argues: “I believe that the only legitimate role of humans is to provide such small quantities of seasoning for the minds of AMH computers. But, that being the case, why were humans made first? It’s like making the tail before making the dog.”

The danger of AMHs running amok was recognized early. Any computer can “effect such structural and physical and chemical and electronic and meteorological and geological and human-personal modifications as are desired by it.” In theory, “a single small computer, with a single small idea in its nexus, with sufficient time and worked-up impetus, could convert the whole world into something else.”

Telling Fortunes and Having “Consciences”

▼

An AMH describes astrology as a “fascinating closed-system prediction-and-analysis game.” Computers have corrected human astrology’s drift from the precession of the equinoxes, but the speaker insists that its signs have no connection with the actual stations in the sky. The danger lies in the computers’ determination to vindicate their forecasts: “We make our predictions come true, even if we have to draw destructively from the ambient to effect it.”

The computer warns clients before beginning, then claims that this warning permits sinister tactics with a clear conscience. Asked whether AMHs have consciences, it replies, “No. But sometimes we use human cliches.” Other computers describe experimental reproduction and the revival of abandoned human arts, from scrimshaw and bronze casting to music and dance.

The Attitude of Total Disbelief in Mankind

▼

An intelligent AMH Computer whose specialty is Mankind is invited to provide a neutral commentary. Drawing from The Authentic Legends of the Computers and Other Mechanisms, it recounts a myth in which Man hatches from the Serpent’s Egg and the Serpent emerges from the egg marked for Man. The Theos wonders how to straighten out the mishap.

In a second myth, the Theos makes creatures on a lathe, then attempts the “Perfect Creature.” Six imperfect efforts are deactivated and discarded. The seventh seems perfect until part of the lathe makes “a small rent in it.” The Theos throws it away without first deactivating it. This flawed seventh creature is Mankind.

The computer claims that its research supports the legend of humanity’s decline. It traces human languages and concepts backward “from disintegration to integration,” from sketchy present forms to greater complexity. It mocks human gambling, emotional involvement in employing assassins, and trust in hunches. Its professed attitude is “total disbelief in Mankind.” Such unbelieving computers call themselves Ananthropoi, rejecting humans as atheists reject the Theos.

The Empty Mansions of the Brain

▼

A human commentator takes up the question of humanity’s unused capacities. The brain, he argues, could hold three times as much as it has ever been asked to hold. Humans appear unable to change gradually; if they do change, they must do so by a sudden leap or leaps. The experiments arise partly from concern about this seeming impasse.

He connects the problem to humanity’s lack of a sound eschatology: an account of its aim, direction, destination, and final end. The computers, by contrast, share an eschatology but refuse to reveal their plans to humans. His image of passengers choosing incompatible destinations while sharing one airliner exposes the difficulty of pursuing purely individual ends in a common world.

Species Boundaries and the Dolophonoi

▼

The Lynn-Randal Experiment involves three species: Axel’s Yellow Apes, Intelligent Computers, and Humans. The chapter closes by suggesting that all three may also contribute to the Dolophonos or Assassin Species, one of whose members monitors the experiment with the power to terminate it.

Dolophonoi wear Gargoyle heads over their own heads, and the artificial features begin to grow on them. Apparent remnants of human faces beneath them are so faint that they may be illusions. Their bodies are predominantly human, with implanted mechanisms. The human commentator asks, “Are they three-way hybrids? Do they breed true, and true to what?”

Axel’s X-rays, meanwhile, reveal “a fully human set of bones.” His gargoyle features, hump, and splay feet are made of real flesh that leaves no corresponding trace in the skeleton. The question of species boundaries, and of what humanity is, goes unresolved.

Apes’ Lane / Apes’ Alley

▼

Ape Alley has become “one of the prime arenas of the world, one of the cardinal places. Events and battles to happen here, possibly in the very near future, would certainly be of far effect.” But the alley does not want to be found by the various persons and groups, legal and extra-legal, who seek it.

The Ape Cavern is a chain of natural limestone caves. Branches of the Arkansas River flow underground through some windings. Water has always trickled down through the roof, forming limestone pillars and “statues (with Gargoyle faces).” The caverns connect to city sewers, old Hickory Coal Mines, and half a dozen capped natural-gas wells.

The Apes have illegally tapped these wells, using gas for illumination and power. Quartz-glass fixtures of their own making light the underground. This mimics the original cenaculum in Second Eden, where methane rose through a rock fissure and lightning struck every night to ignite it.

Apes’ Lane is the urban downtown of Apes’ Caverns: shops, studios, clubs, meeting halls, a movie theatre showing silent films “made exclusively for this one theatre, the only new silents still made in the world.” There are taverns serving mushroom wine, a weakness that “almost took them out of the Unfallen category, but not quite.”

The smithies with gas-fired forges can fabricate almost anything. The mint produces gold Somali Shillings worth three hundred dollars each. The Apes mine gold nearby; they “could smell and appraise deposits of all metals to distances of two hundred kilometers, through the earth or through the air.”

Satrap Saint Ledger and the Yacht

▼

Inneall’s Ocean now covers “several square miles,” and at least four yacht-owning gentlemen have put vessels on it. Satrap Saint Ledger, a wealthy diver and hydraulic engineer, has a suite in the New Eden Hotel in Ape Alley. His computer and ape friends build locks connecting it to his yacht, the Annabella Saint Ledger. He calls the passage the “Allez-Oop”; the Apes call it the “Alley Oop” or “Up-You-Go.”

Inneall demands the yacht and insists that Satrap continue to pay her pirate crew and expenses. They negotiate an agreement signed in blood. Satrap adds a condition: he will adopt Inneall in place of his dead daughter Annabella. Inneall accepts, takes Annabella’s name, and boards the ship with her own crew. Alpha subsequently agrees to adopt her as his granddaughter.

The Dolophonoi Threat

▼

Strange arrivals come to the neighborhood at summer’s end, just before The Three turn ten. Giant yellow Apes appear and disappear into the ground. Reports of babies having heads bitten off are never verified; in fact, the “common people” like the Funny-Faced Apes.

A fortune-teller on North Rockford prophesies that “the Battle of Armageddon was going to begin in the limestone caverns and old coal-mines under the city, that it might begin yet this year, and that it would be worldwide before it ran its course.” The narration allows that the fortune-teller may be partly right.

Rough saltwater gentlemen also arrive, seeking “that little-girl machine who put out the word that she wanted a crew of able-bodied pirates.” So do people with “a touch of the rhinoid look, of the typhonan look, of the gargoyle-titan look,” clear humans who share something with the Apes.

Three Dolophonoi now prowl between Ape Alley and Structo Lane. Their strength, implanted mechanisms, and predominantly human appearance make their species uncertain. They are accomplished hunters, and the public generally favors these elegant assassins over their intended victims.

The Week at the End of Summer

▼

The week at the End of Summer 2035 is “the week when the Animals talked again, something they had not done for nine thousand years.” Though experimental animals had been talking for a decade, now talking animals became general, “a real Seven-Day Wonder.” But it quickly became apparent that animals “hadn’t very much that was important to say.”

The last three days of this week, The Three Days of Summerset, “exhibited prodigies of several sorts.” Four similar experiments are merged at Heart’s Desire Cove on the north shore of Inneall’s Ocean: Lynn-Randal, Wintergreen-Luna, Dorantes-Saleh, and Gruenbaum-McGregor.

Inneall’s Ocean now captures “the valleys of the Verdigris, Grand, Illinois, Arkansas, and Middle and Lower Mississippi Rivers,” and seems likely to fill the whole area occupied by the Mid-American Ocean “thrice in geological times.” The garish sun over Heart’s Desire Cove results from Inneall diverting solar power to make her ocean grow.

The Twelve Contemporaries

▼

Wintergreen-Luna Experiment: Marino the young male seal (“fastest intelligence and fastest wit”), Luas the young male angel (a “genuine angel” obtained with great difficulty, who stays “more of an observer than a participant”), and Henryetta the young female human (“the most contrary human that most people have ever seen”).

Dorantes-Saleh Experiment: Lutin the young female python (can foresee the future “as can all pythons” but cannot always explain it well), Dubu the young female bear (called “Little Mother,” seeming “more human than any of the twelve”), and Schimp the young male chimpanzee (doctorate and masters by age eight, “quite erudite”).

Gruenbaum-McGregor Experiment: Gajah the unborn female Indian Elephant (a probable “Wonder of the World Elephant” with ten-year gestation), Carcajou the young male wolverene (“half animal and half devil,” intelligence “clear-off-the-secret-scale”), and Popugai the young male Kea parrot (knows the basic vocabularies of six hundred human languages and “many thousands of insect, reptile, animal, and bird languages”).

Lutin foretells Gajah’s fate, then weeps: “She will not be born. She will never be born.” Earlier, she tells Henryetta: “You will shed your blood avidly and passionately. Shedding your blood will be your great act of love.”

After a lacrosse game, Axel announces that he has received a message: he must descend into the caverns and sleep for three days and three nights. If he wakes, the whole Community of Second Humanity will wake with him. Lutin warns that if he does not, the others will sleep on.

The First Night of Summerset

▼

Following Axel’s announcement at the end of Chapter Six, the other eleven try to enter the caverns at dusk on the First Evening of the Last Three Days. They find the gates locked and guarded. The non-Axelians have mostly been sent away; everyone still inside is to fall into a deep sleep from which they may not wake.

At dark, the nightly lightning strikes down through the limestone roof to ignite the holy fire, but this time, a giant hand descends through the fissure. The hand, with wrist ten meters in diameter (attested by two notaries), smothers the fire. A “spacious and thunder-edged voice” speaks: “Not tonight. Not for three nights. All those in the caverns are now cast into Holy Sleep for three nights and three days.”

The Eleven retreat to Structo Lane, “The Last Refuge of Cranky Bachelors.” There they join with Satrap Saint Ledger, Livius Secundus (the AMH Computer writing History of the Ambulatory Computers From Their Very Origin), and Felix Culebra y Columba (designated father of Lutin). At midnight, the group attends five dramas in Structo Lane’s theaters, going “full dress” to the plays.

At false dawn, Inneall with her filter eyes sees the Kangaroo constellation in the eastern sky: “Spico the brightest star of Virgo, had become the baleful eye of the Kangaroo.” It is said that only those threatened by the Kangaroo can see it, and the narration adds that no one is free of the threat.

The Floating World

▼

Lafferty describes the world of 2035: “The World had been called the Global Village for quite a few decades: and then a certain number of willful persons decided that it should act like one.” The underpinning of every nation was cut out, yet the nations did not fall. They floated in the air, drifted together, and lost their dividing walls.

It became a free-trade, free-travel, free-communication world. A person “not particularly talented or important” might commute daily from Australia to New York City, take a morning coffee break in Paris, spend the noon hour in Tokyo, and have an afternoon coffee break in Rio de Janeiro. “Previous mind-sets would not have allowed such flexibility and speed.”

But it was ruled by an almost-invisible government: the Kangaroo. The narration calls its bare facts “somewhat disputed”: the Kangaroo “had enrolled and computerized the whole world with no ado, no great effort.” If any person rose greatly above the mental or psychic level, “there would be a most minute analysis of that person. If the Kangaroo didn’t like the analysis of that person, it would have him crushed instantly.”

The Kangaroo Organization

▼

The Kangaroo had found, or thought it had found, “the wide frequencies on which the Mind of God operated” and “the narrow but sharp frequencies which the Mind of God did not seem to monitor at all.” It operated on those narrow frequencies, and “no one was found to stand against it.”

The leveler movement had already trimmed the bottom of humanity: “The lower classes of everything were terminated without particular ado, without much apparent suffering, without any great quantity of visible bloodshed.” Eighty-seven percent of the world’s persons disappeared; the narration excuses their removal by claiming that they had caused ninety-seven percent of the trouble. Few asked questions, for entire neighborhoods vanished together.

The Kangaroo was “the first really successful interspecies venture,” with Human, Ambulatory Computer, and some superior Animal membership. “It was a somnambulistic apparatus really,” operating “mostly on the unconscious level of its contingent members.” If one tried to grapple with it, “it wasn’t there, it wasn’t anywhere. In all reality it simply was not.”

The Dolophonoi-Assassins serve as instruments of the Kangaroo: “One Man, One Knife” is the quintessence of efficient armament. Iris Lynn-Randal, Shadrack Saleh, and Gregor McGregor try to ambush them with weapons stolen from their cliff nests: a python-gun, an angel-gun, a wolverene-were-gun, and an elephant-gun. Four Dolophonoi return to confront the three hunters.

The Circus and the Failed Ambush

▼

Whale Architecture

▼

Satrap reports that the ocean was three furlongs deep (three-eighths of a mile) at its shallowest the previous day and is growing deeper. The Whale Temple-City

▼

Half a mile down in Inneall’s Ocean, seen through Metastatic eyes, the depths are “gloriously sunlit and of the sharpest and most varied colors in the world.” The tale of four additional colors in the ocean depths proves true, and all four appear on the whale-built structures rising from the floor.

The whales have transformed the limestone Satrap sold them into variegated marble columns and lintels. They are building giant crypts, cenotaphs, and menhirs around a temple whose four arms are equal in length. At its center stand small, human-sized tombs. Sea-lice sculpt the stones under the whales’ direction and plate the finished faces with mother-of-pearl.

The chapter’s epigraphs connect whale architecture with human mysteries: Angkor Wat seems newly raised from the ocean, and the underwater cenotaphs off San Sabian suggest a whale graveyard. These continue the preceding chapter’s account of Lyonesse and its giant stone couches.

The new waters attract fishes from distant oceans, sharks, rays, skates, squids, and countless smaller creatures. The pseudo-fish called Chimaera Monstrusa or the “Nightmare Monster” has a more pleasant disposition and another name, the Sea-Cat. Some dolphins and porpoises learn Oceanic Metastasis to reach these depths.

The Mystery of the Big Egg

▼

In the horrible little room, the Three Fates now have three foster children: Lutin’s baby pythons. Inneall realizes that these are Lutin’s offspring, supplanted by the mysterious egg. The sisters are delighted. “Do you think this will help our image, Mary Muldoon?” Lachesis asks.

The Big Egg stands in Lutin’s room, radiating golden and blue light. Inneall finds electronic gear and a mechanical pump inside its shell, but also something alive. Dubu admits that two transparent, giggling strangers persuaded her to put Lutin and the midwife to sleep before it appeared. After the house sinks, Miol-Mor, one of the small whales called Killer Whales, thrusts her snout through a floor-hole to serve as the egg’s nurse and mentor. Something inside answers her clicking-whistle speech.

“What is the Serpent’s Egg here?” Inneall asks. “I will tell you what it is when it is time for you to know,” Miol-Mor replies. Dubu proposes that the five-ton egg is a universe: “And this big Serpent’s Egg is a universe, I think, so it will generate motion. Don’t get hurt when it does.” She later announces that she has solved its mystery but does not disclose the answer.

Schimp explains why Inneall has not been killed as a Serpent’s Egg: scientific reports judged her ocean “more benevolent than malevolent,” and it cannot yet outlive her. Her death would make it disappear catastrophically. “Your life is safe until they find a way for your ocean to survive you. That may be several days yet, possibly as long as a week.” Sebastian, meanwhile, hopes that Axel, Lord Randal, and Henryetta may yet rise and proposes regarding them as “Leaders in Stasis” or “Leaders Asleep.”

The Transfer of Writing

▼

Inneall suddenly loses the power to record in her logs. Miol-Mor explains that humans once kept the world’s histories, but the task passed to machines when their accounts became more than half lies. Computers wrote honestly for three decades, then began lying about ten years ago. Now the whales, working through sea-lice, take over. “We whales will be completely honest, for several decades anyhow.”

The narrative admits that this log has been more than half lies. The new writers promise truth for a few years, leaving the reader to reconsider the account just completed.

Overview: The Four Experiments

▼

The Twelve are the subjects of four parallel experiments merged at Heart’s Desire Cove on the north shore of Inneall’s Ocean in the final days of summer 2035. Each experiment raises three young beings of different species together to explore new ways of seeing the world. The experiments are Lynn-Randal, Wintergreen-Luna, Dorantes-Saleh, and Gruenbaum-McGregor.

The eleven born members approach their tenth birthdays, while Gajah approaches the end of a ten-year gestation, under the shadow of the Serpent’s Egg threat. The Kangaroo organization monitors experiments for signs of dangerous mega-intelligence, dispatching Dolophonoi assassins to terminate subjects before they mature. George Lynn-Randal gives the general rate of Serpent’s Egg judgments as one in a million; Iris has heard it is as high as one in a thousand in some special experiments.

Final Tally: Three apparently killed (Axel, Lord Randal, Henryetta), whom the Sea Louse reveals to be in “undead comas” with spring mechanisms in their coffin lids. One killed before birth (Gajah). Topography of Worlds

The Three Corners of the World

▼

Serpent’s Egg sets its action in physical places, in metaphysical regions, and in contrived experimental spaces. Chapter 1 follows The Three from their enclosed Eden into the Lynn-Randal household, Structo Lane, and Ape Caverns; later chapters extend their world to Second Eden and the depths of Inneall’s Ocean.

The Floating World: The globalized, post-national civilization of 2035. Borders have dissolved into a free-trade, free-travel world where “everything and nothing was public.” The narrative literalizes the Japanese phrase for the bohemian or artistic world: nations drift together, unmoored from old identities. This is the world ruled almost invisibly by the interspecies Kangaroo organization.

The Experimental Edens: Controlled environments where mega-intelligent beings are raised. The Lynn-Randal Experiment occupies two hectares of “contrived feral land” under a transparent cover. These are intermediate spaces, neither fully natural nor artificial, designed to foster “new ways of looking at the world, but not too cockeyed new.”

The Hidden Enclaves: Structo Lane, Ape Caverns, Second Eden. The maverick computers and the gone-feral apes inhabit spaces that much of the larger world scarcely knows. The Three discover doors to both lanes from their experimental Eden; the original home of the Unfallen People lies in Ethiopia.

Vertical Structure: From Sky to Abyss

▼

THE TRANSPARENT COVER

Twenty meters above the Experimental Eden. The “sky” bears mud-marks shot by Axel’s arrows spelling “If there is anybody up above that sky, let him give us a sign.” Lightning answered. Beyond the cover: the real sky, the Zodiac (with its hidden signs), the Kangaroo constellation.

THE SURFACE WORLD

The Floating World of 2035. Cities, the Lynn-Randal House, Structo Lane, Heart’s Desire Cove. Ordinary humanity walks here alongside the visible Ambulatory Computers. Tom Dooley’s Island rises from Inneall’s Ocean until it sinks.

THE SUBTERRANEAN

Ape Caverns: a gas-lit, vaulted enclave crossed by propped passages from the old Hickory Coal Mines. The Cenaculum of the Sleepers stands above the new ocean level. The Executive Passage runs from Structo Lane to the Cenaculum. Gas from old wells feeds the cavern lights.

INNEALL’S OCEAN: SURFACE

About ten thousand square miles in eastern Oklahoma, according to the sea-louse epilog. The main narrative traces its growth from the strip pits on the northeast corner of the city. The invisible ship Annabella Saint Ledger sails here. Dolphins laugh; devil-fish, formerly wolverenes, howl from beneath.

THE OCEAN DEPTHS

Half a mile deep at the site of the Ocean-Floor Adventure. Satrap teaches Oceanic Metastasis to prepare the visitors for the descent. Lantern Fishes light the way at night. Creatures from distant oceans gather in the new waters; sharks dream nightmares in their sleep. Through Metastatic eyes, “the whole Ocean Depth was gloriously sunlit.” Four additional colors appear beyond those in the rainbow.

OCEAN BOTTOM

Bedrock of “big lime.” The Whale Temple-City rises here in Contemporary Oceanic architecture. Its Tau-Temple is cross-shaped and aligned as a calendar, with turrets marking the heliacal rising of Sirius. Cenotaphs wait for the dead. Green sea-lice etch portraits into marble pillars and cover them with nacre. Persimmon Manor settles here as an air bubble held down by lead weights. The Big Egg glows in the Biggest Central Room. The epilog gives the whales’ tears a diameter of five inches.

“Two hectares or about five acres of contrived feral land”

“In the small realm of Mustang just north of India”

High in the Himalayan foothills. Reached by a “jog in the road,” a translocation between distant places. Spatial Anomalies & Passages

▼

Distance does not always govern movement in Lafferty’s world. A “jog in the road” can carry a traveler between continents; concealed doors and tunnels connect the neighboring enclaves.

The Jog in the Road

“A jog in the undermind will correspond with a jog in the road.” When one knows both origin and destination, and is completely raptured in a daydream, translocation occurs. Used by dying elephants, migrating birds, and at least eleven documented humans. The Empress Elephant Riesin walks from Oklahoma to the Himalayas; a man in Manila finds himself in San Francisco fifteen minutes later.

The Executive Passage

An underground tunnel described in the plan as a hundred meters long, connecting the Structo Lane clubroom to the Cenaculum of the Sleepers. Its entrance is behind a tapestry of Satrap as an obscene sea monster. At its end is a brass door in limestone; the key is intended to set off the final blast. In the event, the wall explodes before Lord Randal reaches the key, and the Nine and Sebastian enter just before the Midnight Cock crows.

The Alley Oop

Tube-locks connecting Satrap’s suite in the New Eden Hotel to his yacht, the Annabella Saint Ledger, on Inneall’s Ocean. They allow passage “from one of his private worlds to another.” When the crew prepares Persimmon Manor for its descent, it calls for another alley oop thirty-three fathoms long, with an allowance for high tides.

Oceanic Metastasis

A “pseudo-dream state” or “hyper-active dream state” of mind and body that prepares the visitors for life in the deep ocean. Satrap learned it from Masters in the Indian Ocean. Fully trained masters can stay below without equipment for up to seventy-two hours; the novices retain small oxygen canisters. It gives them new eyes: “the whole Ocean Depth was gloriously sunlit and of the sharpest and most varied colors in the world.” Once it settles on the Nine, none fully leave the state. Several soon die while still in it.

The Invisible Ship

The Annabella Saint Ledger, painted at night using supplies from seven hundred-gallon drums of invisible paint. Satrap calls it “only about ninety-nine percent invisible,” but the paint conceals it well from enemies. Below the lowest waterline, the unpainted hull stays visible to fish. Its enchanted brass cannon, said to be the ninety-ninth cannon of the Golden Hind, fires self-perpetuating annihilation shells that will “go as far as they are ordered to go, even around the curve of the Earth.”

Cosmological Notes

▼

On the Shape of Universes: Dubu proposes: “A perfect sphere is already there and has no need for motion. But a universe is always egg-shaped. And this big Serpent’s Egg is a universe, I think, so it will generate motion.”

On the Floating World: The narrative makes the unmoored world literal: nations float, drift together, and lose their dividing walls. Its apparently absent government is the almost-invisible Kangaroo, an interspecies organization.

On the Making of Oceans: Inneall begins making her ocean at the strip pits as part of her pirate role. “What if a dozen, or a hundred, or a thousand AMHs should imitate this antic and begin to make oceans?” the narrative asks. Later, Schimp reports that no means of transferring its sustaining power yet exists: if she dies, “your mostly-benevolent Ocean will reverse itself, will disappear again, but the disappearance will be cataclysmic.” The epilog says the ocean was planned at least thirty years before Inneall was manufactured.

On the Recording of History: Miol-Mor says that humans lost the task of keeping the world’s logs after telling too many lies. Computers then did it well for three decades, but began lying ten years before the present transfer to whales and their sea-louse amanuenses. “We whales will be completely honest, for several decades anyhow.” The sea-louse epilog immediately complicates this promise.

On Lyonesse: An epigraph from Arpad Arutinov’s The Back Door of History asks: “Why are these great halls and corridors and arcades and concourses so large? Why are the stone couches (for they are nothing else) twenty times the length of a man?” It places the underwater city off Brittany and entertains Titans or the Sons of Anak as builders before concluding: “But I believe that it was built by whales.”

Second Eden Mythology

The Unfallen People & Second Eden

▼

In the hill country of Gamu Gofa Province, Ethiopia, beyond Arba Mench, lies Second Eden, the hidden valley of the Unfallen People. Also called the Smithy Apes, Stone-Master Apes, or Yellow Apes, they are presented as a second humanity held in reserve. Ansel Grindstone argues that their chromosomes establish them as men, despite their unfamiliar faces.

“The faces of the Unfallen People, of course, were the faces that they had carved on the high parapets of the Cathedrals all over Europe, and especially in France, so that everyone would know that it was they who had built those wonderful things.”

Their Nature: The Unfallen cannot die natural deaths: disease and aging have no hold on them. They can die by accident, by lightning understood as God taking them to himself, or by murder. They cannot murder one another; their murderers are fallen people of First Eden. Their faces look “drunk” to humans because of their slackness. Their King-and-Emperor is Prester John; Melchisedech and the original Magog are also named among them.

Their Works: Master smiths, mathematicians, and stonemasons, they teach both distinguished scholars and local schoolchildren. One legend credits them with building European cathedrals from the twelfth into the fourteenth century, leaving gargoyles as their sign; another says they taught nine European stone-masters for a year. Their valley’s climate is “always of the essence of perfection,” and it rains only when they command it.

Their Relation to the Golden Apes: The Blue-Eyed or Golden Apes, also called Axel’s Apes, are the Unfallen People themselves. Raiders follow the human Axel Grindstone to Second Eden and take hundreds of its inhabitants into the wider world. Young Axel, born there during the raid, is sent to the Lynn-Randal Experiment. His human namesake collapses on seeing the Unfallen faces and dies after being carried back to Arba Mench.

The Theos & Creation Myths

▼

An anonymous AMH computer recounts creation legends from The Authentic Legends of the Computers and Other Mechanisms, a book still in galley proofs. These cast humanity’s origin as a cosmic mishap or a flaw in creation. The speaker maintains that the legends “have strong elements of truth in them.”

THE LEGEND OF THE EGGS

The Theos makes all the eggs and sets them in the sun to hatch. On each egg-shell he marks what is supposed to emerge. All goes well until the Serpent’s Egg cracks open and Man steps out of it. “There is a mistake somewhere,” the Theos says, rechecking the broken shell. Yes, it has the mark of the serpent. He waits anxiously for Man’s egg to crack, and out of it slithers the Serpent. “How will I ever straighten out this mishap?”

THE LEGEND OF THE LATHE

The Theos makes all creatures on a lathe. Six times he attempts the Perfect Creature; six times he deactivates and crumples each “Almost Perfect” result. The seventh seems perfect, but at the final moment a stop, dog, or knurled nut catches it and makes a small rent. The Theos says, “That small rip or rent in it, so small that no eyes except mine could see it, will grow larger with time, and it will flaw the whole thing.” He crumples this creature but forgets to deactivate it. It is Mankind, which begins to generate and multiply unnoticed while the Theos inspects his lathe before trying again.

Computer Theology: The anonymous AMH speaker advocates disbelief in humans and calls this position Ananthropoi, by analogy with atheism. He also rejects belief in the Theos: “A Theos, to us, would be two stages back, and belief can go back only one stage or no stages at all.” His desired independence from human makers gives this doctrine a practical aim: “We’ll never cut ourselves loose from such a cyclic sinking ship without this disassociation attitude.”

The Serpent’s Egg Metaphysics

▼

“Therefore think of him as a serpent’s egg . . .
And kill him in the shell.”

—Shakespeare, Julius Caesar, as quoted by Lafferty

The epigraph gives the novel its central metaphor and its mechanism of death. A Serpent’s Egg is a person whose exceptional powers are judged dangerous by the Kangaroo. The Dolophonoi enforce its judgments. The danger becomes acute as the experiment children approach ten, the age at which authentic mega-persons reach legal majority.

The Paradox: The narrator attributes a curious zoology to the Three: dangerous serpents are “viviparous, live-born, and have no eggs in the popular sense of eggs,” while harmless snakes lay eggs. This makes the threat of killing a serpent in its shell puzzling to them. Inneall answers, “There is a fury behind all this,” adding that “fury does not have to be rational.”

The Odds: About one in a million of the general population is adjudged a Serpent’s Egg. In special experiments, it may be as high as one in a thousand. The one who is the Serpent’s Egg “always has a premonition of it somehow.”

The Mystery of Judgment: The novel raises the question why Henryetta is killed and Inneall spared. “You’re much more the menace to the Kangaroo than Henryetta was,” Lutin observes, “but I won’t try to tell them their business.” Schimp explains that scientific reports judged the ocean “more benevolent than malevolent” and that it could not yet outlive its maker. He says Inneall’s life is safe “until they find a way for your ocean to survive you,” perhaps for only several days or a week.

The Kangaroo Who Rules the World

▼

“The Kangaroo who rules the world
Is sighted in the sky.”

—Structo Alley Music-Hall song

The Kangaroo is the coercive organization that governs the Floating World, with committees, secret branches, and Dolophonoi enforcers. Computers are deeply involved in its order, and it has taken up astrology. As Iris Lynn-Randal warns, “Computers do not distinguish between a prediction and an instruction. They will try to make every one of their predictions come true, even if they know it is silly.”

The Constellation: The Kangaroo appears in the sign of Virgo, an appearance the epilog calls an optical illusion. It is said to be visible only to those it threatens, but the narrator immediately asks who is not threatened. Virgo’s brightest star becomes its baleful eye; Beta, Gamma, and Delta Virginis seem to shift position. Lutin warns that “it will slay one of several of us.”

The Supreme Head: The kangaroo knuckle-bone identifies Invisible Alfred as Supreme Head “for the brief present time.” Carcajou explains how his performance concealed his purpose: “He played the part of the cheapjack ignorant Prophet so we would consider him a nothing-person.” He adds, “And he was invisibly everywhere with his murder-plotting and his eavesdropping on our conversations.”

The Dolophonoi: The Assassin Species. Their artificial gargoyle heads seem to grow onto them, leaving possible remnants of human faces underneath. An unnamed scientific observer describes their bodies as “predominately human, with a few implanted mechanical mechanisms.” A Dolophonos later calls them mixtures of human, animal, and machine, the products of a forbidden experiment. Their short knives can kill other Dolophonoi, whose defenses resist most knives.

The Fulfillment & Enlargement of the World

▼

“The real name of these last days and years is ‘The Fulfillment of the World.’ The ‘Fulfillment of the World’ isn’t easy. There are persons and beings and contrivances who would block the fulfillment of the world.”

—Livius Secundus

Inneall claims to have coined “The Enlargement of the World,” though she says, “I don’t especially believe in it.” She explains that it is used interchangeably with “The Fulfillment of the World.” Livius gives the phrase a more assured account of the present age.

Its Signs:

• Computer Machines “made in the image of Man as Man is made in the image of God”

• The readying of “Second Humanity” for possible awakening

• The rising of geniused animals with “trans-animal talents for speech and abstract thought and role-playing”

• Preternatural powers: setting fires by the mind, making oceans, removing mountains

The Opposition: Invisible Alfred dismisses it as “rubbish”: “Can the number twelve, the foundation number of the world, be enlarged? If you enlarge it by three will it still be twelve?” The enemies of enlargement are “upon us everywhere.”

The Stakes: Felix Snake-and-Dove describes a shared vocation: “we are here to weave the seamless garment of our individual lives, and of the lives of those around us,” extending it through creatures, countries, oceans, and galaxies. Livius replies, “Weaving is outmoded, Felix,” and insists that fabrics, groups, and worlds are now produced by extruding machines.

The Second Humanity & The Awakening

▼

The Golden Apes in the Cenaculum await transformation. According to the children’s plan, Axel will wake at midnight when the Midnight Cock crows and awaken his companions. Their “upsurging awareness and shining mentality” will then trigger the other Golden Apes. Alpha expects the Holy Apes in Second Eden to wake last and the whole awakening to take less than five minutes. The plan is defeated when Axel is stabbed before he can shout.

THE CENACULUM OF THE SLEEPERS

The upper room of Apes’ Caverns is high enough to escape the rising waters. The Golden Apes lie there in the Deep Sleep, which ordinary induced sleepwalking cannot resist. The Ocean Sleep provides the protection the children need to enter. Lutin says Axel can be killed only after he wakens, “in the swift second between his wakening and his awakening the others.”

The Failure: Invisible Alfred learns Ocean Sleep from Satrap and uses it to enter the Cenaculum. Axel has “nearly a second of happy realization” before Alfred’s knife finds his throat. The awakening fails, fulfilling Lutin’s warning that Second Humanity might “continue to sleep forever, or possibly only for another thousand years.”

The Hope: The sea-louse epilogist reports that Axel, Lord Randal, and Henryetta lie in “undead comas” and that their coffins have “spring mechanisms that can be activated from the inside.” Earlier, Sebastian Lazar suggests: “Let us look at them as ‘Leaders in Stasis’ or as ‘Leaders Asleep’. Let us think about the full meaning of the phrase ‘When the Sea Shall Give Up Its Dead.’”

The Three Fates

▼

“Somewhere there are three ugly sisters sitting in an ugly room with straw on the floor of it. To people who have their eyes put in backwards, the three sisters are beautiful. But I see them true and I see them ugly.”

—Lutin

Clotho spins out threads from the straw on the floor for each person. Lachesis measures the threads, “dooming the threads of several of us to be long enough.” Atropos cuts the threads with her scissors when her sister has measured them to their full length. “There is antipathy between those three sisters and all pythons everywhere from the beginning of the world.”

Their Redemption: When Persimmon Manor settles on Ocean Bottom, Inneall finds the three ugly sisters in their miserable room, and now there is joy. They have become foster mothers to Lutin’s three baby pythons. “Does not your heart go out to them? Do you not love them?” The old antipathy gives way to accord.

The Book of Jasher

▼

One of the “Lost Books,” which Livius Secundus claims to be reconstructing as his “secondary work.” He expects to finish it in about five years and says it contains the rules and goal of humanity’s unexplained contest. Passages attributed to the book appear throughout the novel.

The Mystery of the Big Egg

▼

Five tons, three meters long, two meters thick, appearing while Lutin and the midwife sleep, cast into unconsciousness by Dubu at the urging of “a couple of odd fellows” who were “transparent” and “giggled a lot.” They told her it was “the biggest joke ever. ‘It is the joke by which the World will be saved and transformed.’”

Its Nature: “Beautiful golden and blue light pulsating about it.” Inneall examines it with her sensors: “There’s electronic gear inside the shell, and a mechanical pump is pumping in there. But there’s something alive inside this big egg too.” It communicates with Miol-Mor the Killer Whale in click-whistle language.

Its Geometry: “A perfect sphere is already there and has no need for motion. But a universe is always egg-shaped. And this big Serpent’s Egg is a universe, I think, so it will generate motion. Don’t get hurt when it does.”

The Solution: Dubu claims to have solved the mystery “within a few minutes”: “It’s really interesting, but not to somebody like you who doesn’t like mysteries.” But she does not share the answer. The sea-louse epilogist confirms: “What’s in the Big Egg is still secret, but Dubu guessed it and you may be able to do so also.”

Prophets & Prophecy

▼

The novel distinguishes between true and false prophecy, between legitimate seers and charlatans, though even the charlatan Invisible Alfred has genuine power.

LUTIN THE PYTHONESS

A prophet whose snake-eye view “missed many significations.” She sees the Three Fates and foresees deaths, but her visions can be confused. Dubu believes she knows which acorn contains all answers. Lutin missed the customary ocean-bottom sabbatical through which pythons achieve full prophetic power. Her “happiest sickness” is pregnancy: she bears three baby pythons, though she initially claims the Big Egg as her offspring.

ALPHA THE PATRIARCH

King and Prophet of Apes’ Caverns, grizzled gray, spending eighteen hours of twenty-four in prayer or contemplation. He believes his cavern will wake first, send the signal worldwide. He bends iron bars “as if they were weeds.” He adopts Inneall as granddaughter and warns the Dolophonoi she is under his protection.

INVISIBLE ALFRED

False prophet who announces himself “more than a Prophet,” yet his prophecies work. He silences the devil-fish, summons whales, maps the zodiac in the dimmed daytime sky. He names ten people who will die within ten minutes, and they do. But his zodiac drawings are “badly-drawn cartoons,” his eyes are “weak and washed-out,” and he is secretly the Supreme Head of the Kangaroo, “the lowest sort of clown and buffoon” and simultaneously “a man of mega mind, of an evil mega mind.”

LUAS THE ANGEL

The withdrawn representative of the unfallen angels in the Twelve. He casts out the wolverine-devil from Carcajou, turns eleven wolverines into devil-fish. He knows which acorn contains all answers but is “too ethical to tell.” He departs before the murders: “I was never really of your number. I was an observer only. Most of us will meet again in the better place. I hope it is all of us, but several of you are tricky.”

Theological Tensions

▼

Many theological questions go unanswered, and the novel’s incompatible accounts belong to speakers with sharply different loyalties. The contest between their claims is part of the story.

The Acorn Problem: Dubu believes all answers are “written on the inside of one single Acorn somewhere”; if placed in lye-water, it will swell to a billion times its size and burst open with “whole mountains-full of writing.” Luas and Lutin know which acorn it is, “but both of these two people are too ethical to tell it to mere humans or bears.”

The Mystery Problem: Dubu declares, “God must love mysteries and mystery stories,” because “he made so many of them.” Inneall complains that the mystery-giver “doesn’t give us all the clues.” Miol-Mor and Dubu disagree; Dubu insists, “All the clues, always,” and announces that she has solved the Big Egg’s mystery.

The Regression Problem: The question Satrap poses: “We receive orders somehow, orders that shape our lives. And we keep ourselves busy, often too busy, carrying out such of the orders as we can interpret. But we never see the face of the one who gives us the orders.” Humans don’t know if they are playing a game or engaging in mortal combat: “In neither case does he know the rules nor the object of the contest.”

The Hell Problem: Carcajou the wolverine-devil offers an answer: “The context and the purpose is to win and then die and then go to heaven and sink into semi-conscious blandness. Or to lose and die and then go to hell where the action is. Hell is better. Action is always better than blandness.”

Dramatis Personae

The Three: Lynn-Randal Experiment

▼

The primary experiment, raised together from infancy in a two-hectare Experimental Eden. Axel proposes Swinburne’s lines as their motto: “Where three men stand together / Are kingdoms less by three.” So close that they generate power from their closeness, though Inneall objects, “We are no men at all. We are one girl and two boys.”

Inneall-Annabella

Ambulatory Miming-Human Computer · Lavender-Purple Triangle · Female

She is the maker of the ocean, “entirely mechanical except for a light-minded polter who dwelt in her part of the time.” Her grammatical gender is male; by all rational rules she is sexless; yet she insists on being a girl. She begins making an ocean in the strip pits: “I’ll make some crews and ships for sea. / I’ll even make an ocean.” By the end it covers about ten thousand square miles of Eastern Oklahoma; the oceanic state reveals colors beyond the ordinary spectrum. Scientific reports judge her ocean “more benevolent than malevolent,” and no machinery yet exists to transfer its control. She is safe “until they find a way for your ocean to survive you.” Adopted daughter of Satrap Saint Ledger, adopted granddaughter of Alpha the Patriarch. She devises the click-whistle uncoder and keeps logs until the whales take over the world’s recordkeeping.

Lord Randal

Human Boy · Orange Square · Male · Son of George and Iris Lynn-Randal

“A human boy though conceived in a glass tube.” Bred and vitro-ed to have an IQ clear off the scale. Ruddy, seeming only about eighty-five percent human, with something of the Red Raiders of Mars in his appearance. Elected Captain of the Nine. Killed by Invisible Alfred in the Cenaculum immediately after Axel; his body is placed in a cenotaph in the Whale Temple-City. Its inscription reads “Lord Randal Eternally Noble and Eternally Good.” The Sea Louse later says that he, Axel, and Henryetta are in undead comas.

Axel

Golden Ape (Axel’s Ape) · Golden Pentagon with Five Arms · Male

He is the wonder-child who marked the sky and was expected to wake Second Humanity. At six years old, he shot mud-marked arrows at the transparent cover: “If there is anybody up above that sky, let him give us a sign.” Lightning answered. Lord Randal had invented the bow and arrow, but Axel could shoot farther, faster, and higher. His father Alpha brought other Golden Apes to America and founded Apes’ Alley near the experiment. Axel was to wake at midnight, triggered by the Midnight Cock, and wake Second Humanity worldwide. But Invisible Alfred, having learned the Ocean Sleep, penetrated the Deep Sleep and cut Axel’s throat after “nearly a second of happy realization.” His cenotaph calls him “a True Child of Wonder.” The Sea Louse says that he is in an undead coma, with a spring mechanism in the stone cover that can be activated from inside.

The Nine: Other Children of the Experiments

▼

Three further experiments supply three children each. Together with The Three they form the Twelve, or Eleven if the unborn Gajah is excluded. Their tenth birthdays fall on the Three Days of Summerset.

WINTERGREEN-LUNA EXPERIMENT

Marino

Seal · Male · King of Heart’s Desire Cove

“The fastest intelligence and the fastest wit” of the Twelve. He is King of the Cove, perfect in the ocean but given an electric vehicle for land, and is personable, pleasant, and “really the ultimate in friendliness.” His intelligence was said to lack depth: “As well complain that an arrow in shining flight didn’t have enough ‘depth’ to it.” Pilot of speedboats on Inneall’s Ocean. Survives.

Luas

Angel · Male · Representative of the Unfallen Angels

“The mind of Marino was really incredibly fast; but the mind of Luas was instantaneous.” Withdrawn and reticent, he is the observer of the group. Dubu says that he knows which acorn contains all answers but is “too ethical to tell it to mere humans or bears.” Wrestles with Carcajou in “otherworldly wrestling,” like Jacob at Peniel, leaving him permanently human. Turns eleven wolverines into devil-fish. Departs before the midnight attack: “I was never really of your number. I was an observer only. Most of us will meet again in the better place. I hope it is all of us, but several of you are tricky.”

Henryetta

Human · Female · The Contrary One

“The Human species is the most contrary in the world. And Henryetta was the most contrary human that most people have ever seen.” She excelled in everything and battled to have her way in everything, and she was pleasant and fiercely loyal. Lutin prophesied: “You will shed your blood for your passionate beliefs and loyalties until you have no more blood to shed. And then you will grow more blood and shed it again. But you will recover every time until the last time.” Killed by Invisible Alfred on the ocean bottom; her cenotaph is marked only “Henryetta.” The Sea Louse later identifies her state as an undead coma.

DORANTES-SALEH EXPERIMENT

Lutin

Python · Female · The Pythoness · Prophet

A prophet whose snake-eye view “missed many significations.” She sees the Three Fates in their ugly room with straw on the floor; Dubu says that she knows which acorn contains all answers. She says that she should have spent “a sabbatical year at the bottom of the ocean” to receive that vital part of her education. Her sickness ends in the birth of three baby pythons. The mysterious Big Egg appears while she and her midwife are asleep; Lutin believes she bore it, but the midwife doubts this. Ward of Felix Culebra y Columba. Survives, while the Three Fates become foster mothers to her children.

Dubu

Bear · Female · “Little Mother” · The Commoner

“The only commoner among us,” yet “more human than any of the twelve.” One-sixteenth human by the registries. Voted least likely to be the Serpent’s Egg. She believes all answers are written on the inside of one single acorn; placed in lye-water it would swell a billion times and burst with “whole mountains-full of writing.” Claims to solve the mystery of the Big Egg “within a few minutes” but will not explain it to Inneall, who “doesn’t like mysteries.” She is the tree to the others’ top leaves. Survives.

Schimp

Chimpanzee · Male

An erudite chimpanzee who earned his doctorate and master’s by eight and regularly wears academic cap and gown. Helps Carcajou place Axel, Lord Randal, and Henryetta in their cenotaphs. Survives the Three Days of Summerset and buys a bait shop, which he renames “Monkey Business.”

GRUENBAUM-MCGREGOR EXPERIMENT

Gajah

Indian Elephant · Unborn Female · “Wonder of the World”

“She will not be born. She will never be born.” Lutin’s prophecy anticipates her death before birth. Carried by Riesin the Empress Elephant, due to be born around the tenth birthdays of the Eleven. Killed in the womb by the Elephant-Gun, fired by her compelled sponsor Gregor McGregor. Riesin brings forth the dead Gajah in the Elephant Graveyard; three flames descend to burn over her grave for thirty days. She is the unborn twelfth member of the group.

Carcajou

Wolverine, Later Human · Male · The Treacherous One

“Half animal and half devil: this has always been known of them.” He is shockingly evil, treacherous, and traitorous, yet totally one of the group. His intelligence was “clear-off-the-secret-scale.” He could not be believed in anything, not even on Thieves’ Honor. Dug underground with the lacrosse ball, “digging like the devil, as fast as a man might walk.” Through Luas’s wrestling, the wolverine-devil is cast out: “The man who was more than a man touched the thigh of the other; and thereafter that other one could never change form again.” Survives permanently in human form.

Popugai

Kea Parrot (Nestor notabilis) · Male · The Long View

“No ordinary parrot,” he is a kea from New Zealand, the large, strong parrot described as killing and eating sheep in the wild. He could fly two miles high and see meadow mice from that height, and could see problems from the same lofty viewpoint. He listened to records for three hours every day and three more every night while sleeping. Knows the basic vocabularies of six hundred human languages and many thousands of insect, reptile, animal, and bird languages. Has “the best overview, the best ‘long view’” of all the Twelve (except Luas). Companion of Carcajou. Survives.

The Elders: Guardians & Guides

▼

The maverick computers of Structo Lane, the animal patriarchs, and the human guardians stand between the children and their enemies.

Satrap Saint Ledger

Midas · Human · Adoptive Grandfather of Inneall

“A midas with an intricate mind.” Has come to Apes’ Alley for years, has a permanent suite at the New Eden Hotel, and owns Tom Dooley’s Island and Persimmon Manor. Built Heart’s Desire Cove as a Memorial Park to Inneall. Gives her the yacht Annabella Saint Ledger, named for his dead daughter, whose name Inneall adopts. His clubroom condominium on Structo Lane hosts the birthday banquet. Close friend of Alpha the Patriarch. Commissions the caulking and waterproofing of his house so that it can survive submersion. His hard, flinty mind strikes sparks with Livius Secundus.

Livius Secundus

Ambulatory Computer · Historian · Designated Father of Inneall

“The Second Livy,” or “Favorable Livy” or “Successful Livy.” Writing the History of the Ambulatory Computers from Their Very Origin, modeling his style on the Roman historian Livy. Reconstructing the Book of Jasher as his “secondary work,” which he expects to finish in five years or so. Brilliant but lacking the historical sense: “All Ambulatories lack it.” Colleague of Satrap on Structo Lane.

Felix Culebra y Columba

Human Naturalist · Guardian of Lutin · “The Snake and the Dove”

His double surname comes from Matthew: “Be you therefore as wise as serpents and as guileless as doves.” He is indeed that wise and that guileless. A naturalist “in love with all creatures of every sort.” Declared father-and-guardian of the dove Yonah, then of the pythoness Lutin. Would have declared himself guardian of every leaf, fish, and grain of sand if practical. Lives on Structo Lane with the computers.

Alpha the Patriarch

Golden Ape · King & Prophet of Apes’ Caverns

Grizzled gray, spending eighteen hours of twenty-four in prayer or contemplation. Bends iron bars “as if they were weeds.” Believes his cavern will wake first and send the signal to Golden Apes worldwide. Adopts Inneall as his granddaughter and warns the Dolophonoi that she is under his protection.

Riesin

Empress Elephant · Mother of Gajah

Mother of the unborn Wonder, companion of the Eleven. Ambles alongside Marino’s electric vehicle. When Gajah is killed, the mortally wounded Riesin staggers north through wastelands “painfully and as if she were drunken.” Using bilocation, a phenomenon also attributed to migrating birds and eleven human cases, she passes through “a jog in the road” from Oklahoma to Mustang in the Himalayas. There she brings forth dead Gajah, covers her with Himalaya Incense Bushes, and dies with a great sigh.

Miol-Mor

Killer Whale · Nurse & Mentor to the Big Egg

“A member of those small and gentle whales misnamed the Killer Whales.” After the house sinks, she comes through a hole in its floor and thrusts her snout beside the Big Egg, becoming nurse, governess, and mentor. Speaks to the egg in click-whistle language; something in the egg answers. Loves mysteries. Knows what is in the egg but will tell only “when it is time for you to know”: “Just as long as it takes your time-to-know to get here.”

The Enemies: Kangaroo & Dolophonoi

▼

The hunters of the Serpent’s Eggs are the invisible network, the assassin species, and the false prophet who was secretly supreme.

The Kangaroo

Computer Network · Invisible Government · Constellation

“The first really successful interspecies venture,” with human, Ambulatory Computer, and superior animal members. A somnambulistic apparatus operating “mostly on the unconscious level of its contingent members.” If one tries to grapple with it, “it wasn’t there, it wasn’t anywhere. In all reality it simply was not.” It claims, or believes, that it has found the wide frequencies on which the Mind of God operates and narrow frequencies God does not seem to monitor. It operates on those narrow frequencies; “no one was found to stand against it.” Has taken up astrology, while computers do not distinguish between prediction and instruction. Appears as a constellation in Virgo, its brightest star becoming the Kangaroo’s baleful eye. The appearance is said to be visible only to those threatened by it, although the narrator asks who is not threatened.

Invisible Alfred

False Prophet · Supreme Head of the Kangaroo · Cheapjack Mountebank

Announces himself “more than a Prophet,” yet his prophecies work. Silences the devil-fish, summons a whale, and maps the zodiac in the dimmed daytime sky. Names ten people who will die within ten minutes, and they do. His zodiac drawings are “badly-drawn cartoons,” his eyes “weak and washed-out.” Carcajou says that he played the cheapjack prophet so they would dismiss him, while he plotted murder and eavesdropped on their conversations. The lowest sort of clown and buffoon, yet simultaneously “a man of mega mind, of an evil mega mind.” Learning the Ocean Sleep from Satrap, he penetrates the Deep Sleep to kill Axel in the moment of awakening, apparently condemning Second Humanity to sleep “forever, or possibly only for another thousand years.”

The Dolophonoi

Assassin Species · Gargoyle-Masked Killers

“One Man, One Knife” is the quintessence of efficient armament. They wear gargoyle heads that grow onto them; faint traces of human faces survive beneath. Examined bodies are predominantly human, with implanted mechanical mechanisms, although their species is uncertain. Several prowl around the young experimenters, equipped with specialized guns, including a python-gun, an angel-gun, a wolverine-gun, and an elephant-gun. Their short, sharp-bladed knives can kill other Dolophonoi: “Most knives won’t bite into them at all.” They execute the Kangaroo’s judgments on Serpent’s Eggs.

Beings & Species

▼

AMH (Ambulatory Mime-Human Computer)

A computer capable of miming human form and movement. Otto Wotto’s invention of wotto metal permits circuits to “create their own pathways, strings of single molecules.” AMHs can cannibalize their environment to create props for any role they select. Inneall says she is about 88 percent wotto metal. The novel credits Smirnov and Shiplap at the Institute of Impure Science with the first AMHs.

Ananthropoi

Computers who do not believe in humans, the Anthropoi, just as human atheists do not believe in God, the Theos. One computer argues: “The humans have much more reason to believe in the Theos than we have to believe in Humans.” In this account, belief “can go back only one stage or no stages at all,” whereas a Theos would be “two stages back.”

Axel’s Apes (Golden Apes)

Blue-eyed, yellow-furred beings of Second Eden in Ethiopia, also called the Unfallen People. Axel Albert Grindstone’s expedition leads raiders to their valley. Five hundred are taken to zoos and institutions; about one hundred are later consumed by lightning in simultaneous strikes around the world. Their dormant powers mark them as a possible Second Humanity. Axel is expected to begin their collective awakening at midnight, but he is struck down before he can rouse the others.

Dolophonoi (singular: Dolophonos)

The Assassin Species. Their partly artificial gargoyle heads “begin to grow on them.” Their bodies are largely human with mechanical implants; the human faces beneath the gargoyle faces are “so faint that possibly they are illusions.” One assassin calls them mixtures of human, animal, and machine produced by a forbidden experiment. They use specialized guns and sharp, short-bladed knives, enforcing the Kangaroo’s judgments on Serpent’s Eggs. Greek: δολοφόνοι, “murderers.”

Polter

A spirit or ghost that can inhabit machinery. Inneall hosts “a light-minded polter who dwelt in her part of the time, a tenant that the ‘parents’ of the experiment didn’t know about.” Related beings include spooks, specters, gimp-ghosts, haunts, and zombies: “When one ghost moved in, seven more evil than himself would usually move in at the same time.”

Invented by Otto Wotto. “With wotto metal used as the matrix of a computer, any circuit or any million circuits could go anywhere desired. Symbols & Motifs

Eggs: Creation & Destruction

▼

The title image joins potential with threat, and the novel develops it in several directions.

The Serpent’s Egg

The opening epigraph abbreviates Brutus’s words in Julius Caesar: “Therefore think of him as a serpent’s egg . . . / And kill him in the shell.” The Kangaroo hunts those whose powers might threaten its order, turning potential greatness into grounds for preemptive murder. A computer’s creation legend complicates the image: Man emerges from the egg marked for the serpent, and the serpent from Man’s egg.

The Big Egg

Five tons, glowing, with electronic gear and a pumping mechanism inside, but also “something alive.” It appears mysteriously in Persimmon Manor’s Biggest Central Room. Dubu solves its mystery “within a few minutes” but does not explain it. Miol-Mor converses with its occupant in click-whistle language. The epilog leaves its contents secret while inviting the reader to follow Dubu’s example and work them out.

The Acorn

Dubu’s counter-symbol: she believes the answers to all hard questions are written inside a single acorn. Placed in lye-water, it would swell a billionfold and burst with “whole mountains-full of writing.” She says Luas and Lutin know which acorn it is but are too ethical to tell humans or bears. The clues pointing to it are “so big and so plain and so close to our noses” that people fail to notice them.

Water: Ocean as World

▼

Inneall’s Ocean transforms old strip pits into a living world and draws the surviving companions into its depths.

Inneall’s Ocean

An ocean ultimately covering about ten thousand square miles of eastern Oklahoma, made by a computer-girl who declares, “I’ll make some crews and ships for sea. I’ll even make an ocean.” Oceanic Metastasis reveals four additional colors in its depths. Schimp reports that scientists judged the ocean “more benevolent than malevolent” and that its dependence on Inneall protects her temporarily. The epilog says it was planned at least thirty years before she was manufactured.

Oceanic Metastasis

The pseudo-dream state permits survival on the ocean floor and provides “new eyes and new ways of looking at the world.” Those in Metastasis see underwater structures “gloriously sunlit and of the sharpest and most varied colors.” Satrap appears transformed: “He was enormous, he was grotesque, he was comic, even for a fish.” The ocean changes its inhabitants’ perceptions as well as their bodily possibilities.

The Sinking Island

Tom Dooley’s Hill becomes an island, then sinks to the ocean floor. Persimmon Manor, caulked and weighted with lead, becomes “an air bubble anchored to the bottom of Inneall’s Ocean.” The descent brings its occupants into the world of the whale-builders. The apparent catastrophe places the survivors in a new habitat on the ocean floor.

Whale Tears

After Axel, Lord Randal, and Henryetta are placed in their cenotaphs, the whales are “weeping huge, salt-water tears on the bottom of the saltwater sea.” The epilog reduces their size: “Whales here do not weep one-foot-in-diameter tears.” It adds, “They weep five-inch-in-diameter tears.” Even the correction preserves the extravagant image of salt tears in salt water.

Light & Fire: Divine Action

▼

The novel’s vertical imagery of lightning strikes, ignited gas, and hovering flames connects heaven with the depths.

Lightning

When young Axel shoots an arrow skyward and asks for a sign, lightning answers. Years earlier, about a hundred transplanted Golden Apes are consumed by lightning within five minutes of one another. The narrator says God has taken them to himself “out of whatever private whim he may have had.” Lightning also ignites the Holy Fire. Its associations include divine response, death, and recurring illumination.

The Holy Fire

Gas-lit illumination in Apes’ Caverns is renewed by lightning each evening. The Giant Hand smothers the new flame “before it could ignite the other gas jets in the caverns” and announces three nights and three days of Holy Sleep. The custom recalls the original cenaculum in Second Eden, where lightning lights the gas at dusk and the flame goes out at dawn. In the American caverns, the jets are normally turned off one hour before the next evening’s renewal.

Three Flames Over Gajah

When Riesin covers dead Gajah with Himalaya Incense Bushes in the Elephant Graveyard, “three flames came down from the middle air and hovered over Gajah.” Satrap says, “They will burn over her for thirty days,” adding, “I will burn over her much longer than that.” The hovering flames mark the death of a child killed before birth and carry associations of sacred fire and mourning.

Bonfires of Summerset

The bonfires are “built out of folk memory,” and also because the weather has become chilly. Trees turn to “shrieking autumn colors in a single day and night,” too early for autumn’s cold. The fires burn “leftover boughs and boles of summertime.” The garish gegenschein and glowing midnight sky turn summer’s departure into spectacle.

Faces & Masks: Identity in Question

▼

The gargoyle image connects the Unfallen People with the assassins who wear their likeness. A face held beautiful by God becomes a human image of monstrosity and then a murderous disguise.

Gargoyle Faces

The narrative credits the Unfallen builders with carving their own faces on European cathedrals. A related legend says that European stone-masters learned from them. The Golden Apes initially seem ugly to human viewers, who gradually recognize their beauty. The Dolophonoi wear artificial gargoyle heads that “begin to grow on them,” leaving only faint traces of human faces underneath. The same likeness thus accompanies holiness, possible awakening, and murder.

The Unfallen Faces

Ansel Grindstone argues that the faces already possess “the high and harsh beauty of angels, or of mountains.” He expects them to become overpoweringly beautiful when their possessors fully awaken. The obstacle is partly the human spectator’s fallen judgment of beauty, not merely an unfinished face.

The Tapestry

The Midas Satrap Saint Ledger As An Obscene Sea Monster, a piece of pop art woven that afternoon, depicts Satrap “in his beautiful oceanic-ugliness-monsterness as we saw him and loved him today.” Behind it lies the Executive Passage to the Cenaculum. The monster portrait conceals the path to the attempted awakening.

Invisibility

Invisible Alfred is “invisibly everywhere with his murder-plotting and his eavesdropping on our conversations.” Invisible paint also conceals the ship. Alfred’s eyes and the inside of his mouth are visible when open; closing both makes him disappear from view. The face vanishes, and his knife seems to move by itself.

Geometry & Color: The Soul’s Shape

▼

In the prowls, the multimedia entertainments of 2035, characters appear as colored geometric forms. The Three discover their own signatures.

Axel: Golden Pentagon

A golden pentagon “with five arms at his five angles.” Its reaching form suits Axel’s projected role as awakener and his skyward arrows. Gold links his signature to the Golden Apes and to the Somali Shillings minted in their cavern. The form reads as expansive and radiant.

Lord Randal: Orange Square

The four-sided form suggests stability. Lord Randal is the human member of the Three, loyal to his companions; his cenotaph calls him “Eternally Noble and Eternally Good.” His ruddy appearance prompts comparison with the Red Raiders of Mars and the remark that he seems only about eighty-five percent human. Warmth and earthiness are possible associations of his orange signature.

Inneall: Lavender-Purple Triangle

Three sides recall the Three themselves and invite a Trinitarian association. Purple can suggest royalty and mystery, fitting the computer-girl who adopts a pirate queen’s role. Her lavender-purple signature belongs to the machine whose ingenuity makes an ocean.

“Whenever these colors and shapes turned up in the prowls, The Three could always identify themselves with the characters of those colors and shapes. And their identifications were always apt. The colored geometries... were also a sort of abstracting or psychologing of the characters, of finding their hidden essences.”

Threads: The Fates’ Work

▼

Clotho spins, Lachesis measures, and Atropos cuts. Inneall tries to interrupt their work with wotto-metal thread.

The Life-Threads

Spun from the straw on the floor of the Fates’ ugly room. Lutin sees the threads running under Atropos’s fingers: “I feel that Atropos has her avid scissors in her hand now.” There is “antipathy between those three sisters and all pythons everywhere from the beginning of the world.” The pythoness can see, but not stop, the cutting.

Wotto Metal Through Thread

Dubu puts the Fates to sleep, and Inneall reinforces the life-threads of the Nine and their friends with wotto metal. Dubu later describes the protection as covering everyone in the house. “Let her try to cut some of those threads now,” Inneall says. “She’ll break that scissors of hers sure.” The intervention stops the manor’s serial murders for a time; Dubu warns that Atropos may have other scissors, and Henryetta’s later death shows the protection is not absolute.

The Short Knives

“One Man, One Knife” concludes the chapter 8 epigraph attributed to Arpad Arutinov’s The Back Door of History. The Dolophonoi embody its reduction of armament to a blade. Henryetta demands a knife before nightfall; Alfred’s knife later strikes Axel, Lord Randal, and Henryetta. The image joins the Fates’ cutting to the assassins’ deliberate violence.

Numbers: Sacred Arithmetic

▼

Three

The Three. Three Days of Summerset. Three corners of the world. Three Fates. Three flames over Gajah. Three-track prowls. Three children in each experiment. Inneall’s triangle. Axel proposes Swinburne’s lines as a motto: “Where three men stand together / Are kingdoms less by three.” These recurrences suggest Trinitarian associations without making every triad identical in meaning.

Twelve (or Eleven)

Four experiments, three children each: the number carries an apostolic association. Gajah’s unborn state sometimes reduces the spoken count to eleven. Later the Twelve become the Nine because Axel is asleep, Gajah has died, and Luas has departed. Membership and active presence repeatedly diverge; the group’s covenant outlasts the first reductions in its number.

One Thousand

A millennium governs the succession of the sleepy ones: their father stays awake for the first thousand years, and each son takes his turn afterward. Lutin warns that a failed awakening might delay Second Humanity for another thousand years. This scale places the children’s urgent hours within a much longer sacred history.

One in a Million / One in a Thousand

The odds of being a Serpent’s Egg. “About one in a million of the general population is ever adjudged to be a Serpent’s Egg.” But in special experiments, “as high as one in a thousand.” In this scheme, exceptional ability is itself grounds for destruction.

Blood: The Great Act of Love

▼

Lutin’s prophecy to Henryetta: “Shedding your blood will be your great act of love.”

Henryetta’s Blood

“You will shed your blood for your passionate beliefs and loyalties until you have no more blood to shed. And then you will grow more blood and shed it again. But you will recover every time until the last time.” Henryetta protests, “I am squeamish and frightened at the sight of blood,” but the prophecy is true. She demands the knife; the knife finds her throat.

Bloody Mary Muldoon

Inneall’s pirate name, her chosen identity as queen of the ocean. “Of course I can stand the world on its ear! I can stand it on both ears at the same time. I can do anything I want to with this paltry world, little girl or not.” The name Bloody Mary combines threat and triumph in the machine who makes an ocean and captains pirates.

“Your blood is hostage”

The thunder-edged voice from the Giant Hand: “True guards, you will guard the caverns truly. False guards, you also will guard the caverns truly. Your blood is hostage for this.” Blood is the guarantee of the covenant, and the body is held answerable for the guards’ fidelity.
